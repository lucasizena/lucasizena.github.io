Here's an instructive problem that uses a clever trick using power of a point[^pop-handout] that I learned today:

{% <statement kind="Problem" title="IMO 2009/2" label="IMO-2009-2"> %}
Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcenter $O$. The points $P$ and $Q$ are interior points of the sides $\overline{CA}$ and $\overline{AB}$, respectively. Let $K$, $L$, and $M$ be the midpoints of the segments $BP$, $CQ$, and $PQ$, respectively, and let $\Gamma$ be the circle passing through $K$, $L$, and $M$. Suppose that the line $PQ$ is tangent to the circle $\Gamma$. Prove that $OP = OQ$. 
{% </statement> %}

The diagram looks something like this:

Well, we'll probably have to use similar triangles in some way to get to the required condition. We'll also probably have to find out why it's important that $K$, $L$, $M$ are midpoints. With this in mind, we notice that we can use this by drawing in segments $LM$ and $KM$, which are actually the midlines of $\triangle PQC$ and $\triangle PQB$, respectively. This is good because we now have that $LM \parallel AC$ and $MK \parallel AB$ by definition, and we could maybe exploit this to do some angle chasing for whatever reason. 

Alright, that's that condition out of the way. What now? We notice that $\triangle PQC \sim \triangle MQL$, and $\triangle QBP \sim \triangle MKP$, and after staring for a bit more, we notice (at least if you've drawn a good diagram) that $\triangle APQ \sim \triangle MKL$, and we can easily prove this by using the midlines we found earlier! That's nice, but how is it useful? We come back to our initial assumption that the required condition probably follows from similar triangles, but it's not really clear how $OP$ and $OQ$ are in any way related to the ones we found. And this is where the clever trick comes in: **notice that the condition required, $OP = OQ$, is equivalent to $OP - R^2 = OQ - R^2$!** In other words, we are equivalently required to show that 

$$\operatorname{Pow}\msub{(ABC)}(P) = \operatorname{Pow}\msub{(ABC)}(Q).$$

Now that we've rephrased the conclusion as $P$ and $Q$ having equal power with respect to the circumcircle, we see that we just need to show that 
$$PA \cdot PC = QA \cdot QB,$$
which follows after some straightforward manipulation of the side ratios we obtain from the similar triangles we found, and we're done!

Here's the full written-up solution:

{% <statement kind="Solution"> %}
Rephrasing the required condition, we can equivalently prove that $OP^2 - R^2 = OQ^2 - R^2$, that is, $\operatorname{Pow}\msub{(ABC)}(P) = \operatorname{Pow}\msub{(ABC)}(Q)$.

First, note that $\overline{ML} \parallel \overline{AC}$ since $\overline{ML}$ is a midline of $\triangle PQC$. Similarly, $\overline{MK} \parallel \overline{AB}$ because $\overline{MK}$ is a midine of $\triangle PQB$. We now claim:
{% <statement kind="Claim"> %}
We have 
$$\frac{PA}{QA} = \frac{KM}{LM}.$$
{% </statement> %}
{% <proof> %}
It's sufficient to show that $\triangle APQ \overset{-}{\sim} \triangle MKL$, which we'll do by angle chasing. We have
$$
\begin{aligned}
\measuredangle MLK &= \measuredangle QMK &\text{alternate segment theorem} \\\\
&= \measuredangle PQA &\text{alternate interior angles.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Similarly,
$$\measuredangle LKM = \measuredangle LMP = \measuredangle APQ.$$
Hence, the claim follows by AA. 
{% </proof> %}
We now claim:
{% <statement kind="Claim"> %}
We have 
$$\frac{KM}{LM} = \frac{QB}{PC}.$$
{% </statement> %}
{% <proof> %}
On the one hand, we have that $\triangle PQC \overset{+}{\sim} \triangle MQL$, so 
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{PC}{LM} = \frac{PQ}{MQ} = 2 \qquad \text{since $PQ = 2MQ$.}
\end{aligned}
$$
On the other hand, $\triangle QBP \overset{+}{\sim} \triangle MKP$, and thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{QB}{KM} = \frac{PQ}{MP} = 2 \qquad \text{since $PQ = 2MP$.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The claim now follows by equating the two and rearranging. 
{% </proof> %}
Combining these two claims, we have that
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{PA}{QA} = \frac{KM}{LM} &= \frac{QB}{PC} 
\iff PA \cdot PC = QA \cdot QB,
\end{aligned}
$$
as desired.
{% </statement> %}
